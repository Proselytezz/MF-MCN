 a b s t r a c t
Self-supervised learning (SSL), which leverages large amounts of unlabeled data to reduce dependence on exten-
sive annotations, has demonstrated remarkable transferability in medical image segmentation tasks. However, 
most existing SSL approaches for medical imaging are limited to single-modality data, overlooking the intrinsic 
complementarity among diﬀerent imaging modalities. In brain tumor analysis, multimodal magnetic resonance 
imaging (MRI) plays a crucial role in accurately delineating tumor subregions, as each modality provides dis-
tinct yet complementary information about tumor morphology and physiology. Therefore, eﬀectively exploiting 
multimodal information during SSL has emerged as an important research focus for advancing brain tumor 
segmentation. In this study, we introduce a novel SSL framework, Modality Fusion and Masked Complemen-
tary Modeling (MF-MCM), developed for multimodal brain MRI. MF-MCM ﬁrst performs modality-wise fusion 
through weighted sum of the input modalities to generate fused images equal in number to the input modalities. 
Subsequently, it applies complementary masking to the multichannel image constructed by concatenating the 
fused images, ensuring that the unmasked regions across all channels are non-overlapping and they jointly occupy 
the whole brain. Finally, by optimizing the multimodal image reconstruction and fusion coeﬃcients regression 
tasks, the pre-training model is endowed with the ability to perceive ﬁne-grained neuroanatomical details, inte-
grate multimodal information, and achieve eﬀective cross-modality interaction. The proposed SSL framework is 
ﬁne-tuned and evaluated on four brain tumor segmentation datasets and one stroke lesion segmentation dataset. 
Results demonstrate that MF-MCM outperforms various state-of-the-art medical SSL methods while eﬀectively 
reducing annotation requirements and maintaining robustness in scenarios where certain modalities are absent. 
Codes and pre-trained models will be available at https://github.com/liangjianwen01/MF-MCM.

ferred diagnostic tool in neurology due to its superior soft tissue con-
trast, non-invasive nature, and avoidance of ionizing radiation. In clin-
ical practice, multimodal MRI plays a vital role in providing compre-
hensive characterizations of tissue alterations within brain tumor re-
gions. The key MRI modalities used in brain tumor analyses include T1-
weighted (T1w), T2-weighted (T2w), contrast-enhanced T1-weighted

Self-supervised learning (SSL) has emerged as an eﬀective paradigm 
for exploiting large-scale unannotated data that are readily available 
but frequently underutilized in practice. By designing and optimizing 
pre-text tasks, SSL aims to learn meaningful and generalizable represen-
tations without the need for extensive manual labeling [4]. Depending 
on the pre-text task, SSL can be broadly categorized into discriminative 
and generative approaches.
Discriminative methods mainly include predictive [5] and con-
trastive learning (CL) approaches [6]. Predictive methods train mod-
els by predicting image transformations, such as image rotation angles, 
image patch indices, and so on. CL methods construct similar and dis-
similar instances, pulling similar samples close and pushing dissimilar 
ones away in the feature space. Although discriminative methods have 
achieved remarkable success in the natural image domain due to their 
ability to recognize global discriminative features, they face challenges 
when applied to medical images, especially brain MRI. In medical imag-
ing, the diﬀerence between diseased and healthy samples is often subtle, 
primarily manifesting in local variations, and discriminative methods 
may overlook these ﬁne-grained characteristics [7]. CL further exacer-
bates this issue, as most CL methods discriminate between positive and 
negative samples at the individual level. However, many anatomical 
structures exhibit highly similar phenotypes across subjects, and treat-
ing samples from diﬀerent individuals as negative pairs fails to account 
for such cross-subject anatomical similarity.
On the other hand, generative methods aim to model the data dis-
tribution, particularly those based on image restoration, which are ef-
fective in capturing and preserving image details and have been widely 
applied in computationally intensive medical image segmentation tasks. 
Speciﬁcally, context restoration methods ﬁrst apply various types of dis-
tortions to an image of interest, such as rotation [8], deformation [9], or 
masking [10–12], and then reconstruct the original image at the pixel 
or voxel level. Context restoration methods may overly focus on im-
age details, making them sensitive to image noise [13]. Multitask SSL 
methods, which combine discriminative and generative tasks, can simul-
taneously capture the overall structure of an image while retaining its 
ﬁne-grained features. Such types of approaches have been extensively 
explored in medical image segmentation, achieving remarkable results.
Current SSL methods for medical imaging predominantly concen-
trate on single-modal imaging. When handling multimodal MRI, existing 
techniques typically process them as multichannel volumes while failing 
to capture critical cross-modal interactions. The eﬀective integration of 
information from each modality is crucial for downstream tasks such as 
brain tumor segmentation [14], as each modality oﬀers unique insights 
into the characteristics of brain tumors. Common strategies for model-
ing cross-modal relationships include joint representations [15] project-
ing individual modalities into separate embedding spaces, disentangled 
representations [16] decomposing modalities into anatomy-preserving 
and appearance-speciﬁc components, and multimodal fusion [17] with 
learnable feature integration mechanisms. As a result, these methods 
fail to capture the underlying anatomical consistency between modali-
ties. While some studies such as M3AE [18] and BrainMVP [19], have 
explored SSL for multimodal brain MRI, they primarily rely on learnable 
ﬁxed templates to align heterogeneous inputs into a shared coordinate 
space. However, such "static alignment" strategies may struggle with the 
substantial anatomical and imaging heterogeneity of brain tumors, as a 
ﬁxed template cannot easily adapt to patient-speciﬁc tumor morpholo-
gies or varied contrast distributions across individuals.
In this paper, we propose a multitask SSL paradigm named Modality 
Fusion and Masked Complementary Modeling (MF-MCM), which shifts 
the focus from static alignment to dynamic cross-modal reasoning. Un-
like previous methods that rely on ﬁxed templates, MF-MCM employs 
innovative Modality Fusion (MF) and Masked Complementary Modeling 
(MCM) strategies to explicitly model inter-modal synergies. During pre-
training, MF-MCM applies modality fusion and complementary mask-
ing for the input data, and then reconstructs the original multimodal 
images and regresses out the fusion coeﬃcients. Restoring multimodal 
brain MRI enables the model to learn not only the intensity distribution 
of each modality, but also the cross-modal interactions. Predicting the 
fusion coeﬃcients enables the model to capture the overall style of each 
modality by identifying the contribution of each modality from a fused 
image. In this way, MF-MCM remains robust to data scarcity and miss-
ing modality in downstream tasks. In summary, our main contributions 
are ﬁve-fold:
• We introduce MF-MCM, a novel SSL paradigm that explicitly models 
cross-modal interactions in 3D multimodal brain MRI without rely-
ing on rigid, modality-speciﬁc templates.
• We introduce Masked Complementary Modeling (MCM), a novel 
strategy that establishes cross-modal bridges by forcing the network 
to recover masked regions using information from complementary 
modalities.
• We introduce the Modality Fusion (MF) strategy, coupled with a co-
eﬃcient regression task, to enhance data diversity and improve the 
model’s ability to decouple shared and modality-speciﬁc features.
• We quantitatively and qualitatively validate our method’s perfor-
mance on downstream segmentation tasks across ﬁve brain MRI 
datasets, including GLI [20], SSA [21], PED [22], MET [23], and 
ATLAS2 [24], demonstrating that MF-MCM outperforms SOTA med-
ical SSL algorithms on both Dice and distance-based metrics (HD95 
and ASSD).
• MF-MCM demonstrates strong data eﬃciency and robustness to miss-
ing modalities, achieving superior segmentation performance even 
with limited annotated data or incomplete MRI modalities.
This paper extends our previous work [25] in three aspects:
• Building upon the foundation of Masked Modality Completion Mod-
eling (MMCM), we introduce modality fusion. This strategy enables 
the model to learn interactions across modalities while eﬀectively 
capturing the distinctive features of each modality.
• We extend our SSL method from single-task learning to multitask 
learning by simultaneously incorporating a regression task for each 
modality’s fusion coeﬃcient.
• We validate the performance of MF-MCM in a broader range of brain 
segmentation scenarios, including glioma tumors from various popu-
lations and brain lesions. We also conduct more comprehensive anal-
ysis experiments.
2.  Related work
2.1.  Self-supervised learning for natural images
SSL leverages the input data itself for supervisory signals and extracts 
rich semantic features inherent in the data, achieving overwhelming suc-
cess in computer vision. For natural images, mainstream SSL paradigms 
include discriminative and generative approaches.
Discriminative SSL is mainly implemented in two forms. One is to 
predict transformations applied to the input data [5]. The other is to in-
dicate whether a sample is positive or negative relative to a data anchor, 
for example, studies [6] construct anchors and positive samples through 
data augmentation, treating other samples in the same batch as the neg-
ative samples for training image encoders. To prevent model collapse, 
methods like BYOL [26] and SimSiam [27] rely solely on anchor and 
positive sample pairs.
SSL methods based on context restoration refer to a class of genera-
tive methods that focus on training encoder-decoder networks to recon-
struct distorted images at the pixel or voxel level. For example SimMIM 
[12] performs pixel-level reconstructions with high masking ratios. BEiT 
[28], inspired by masked language modeling techniques such as BERT

tokens and perform reconstructions from distorted images. SparK [31] 
introduces a BERT-style method based on convolutional neural networks

ple, MixMAE [33] extends the MAE framework by substituting masked 
patches with image patches from another image, subsequently recon-
structing both images. MixedAE [34] reconstructs the mixed images like 
MixMAE does while also introducing an auxiliary source identiﬁcation 
task. These methods, focusing on pixel- or voxel-level information, eﬀec-
tively capture ﬁne-grained visual details and demonstrate exceptional 
transfer performance in downstream image segmentation tasks.
2.2.  Self-supervised learning for medical images
In the recent few years, SSL has achieved substantial success in med-
ical image analysis. Models Genesis [35] trains a network to restore im-
ages corrupted through in-painting, out-painting, nonlinear transforma-
tions, and local shuﬄing. Tao et al. [36] pre-train a network by solving 
the Rubik’s cube game.
Some works try to incorporate medical priors during the pre-training 
phase to ensure that the learned visual representations closely align with 
anatomical structures. ASA [37] incorporates the symmetry of healthy 
brain structures to pre-train a network. TransVW [38] constructs visual 
words based on the anatomical consistency across individuals and im-
proves Models Genesis through self-discovery and self-classiﬁcation pre-
text tasks. SSiT [4] proposes a saliency-guided SSL method to capture 
salient features for diabetic retinopathy grading.
More recently, several studies have incorporated discriminative tasks 
into image restoration to enhance the models’ ability to capture global 
features. For example, SwinMM [39], inspired by multi-view learning, 
enforces consistency of both reconstruction results and high-level se-
mantic features of images from diﬀerent perspectives. PCRLv2 [40] fo-
cuses on preserving visual information through multi-scale pixel restora-
tion and feature comparison. Tang et al. [8] pre-train Swin UNETR [41] 
using a multitask learning approach that combines image restoration, 
image rotation prediction, and contrastive learning tasks. Inspired by 
these studies, we also integrate discriminative tasks to further enhance 
our model’s ability to capture both local and global features. Integrat-
ing Mix-Up into the pre-training of volumetric medical images has also 
shown considerable eﬀectiveness. Wang et al. [42] introduce a strat-
egy called Volume Fusion (VF), which fuses patches from diﬀerent in-
stances and pre-trains a network by predicting the fusion coeﬃcients of 
each voxel. While existing Mix-Up or whole-volume fusion approaches 
primarily focus on sample-level mixing across diﬀerent subjects to en-
hance robustness against inter-patient variability, our approach fun-
damentally diﬀers by applying fusion across diﬀerent MRI modalities 
within the same subject. Furthermore, rather than serving merely as a 
data augmentation technique, our Modality Fusion acts as a specialized 
representation-learning pre-text task. By explicitly regressing the fusion 
coeﬃcients, the network is forced to quantify the exact contribution of 
each modality within the fused input.
2.3.  Self-supervised learning for multimodal images
Multimodal medical imaging oﬀers a great opportunity to compre-
hensively analyze anatomical structures of interest by highlighting their 
diﬀerent tissue components, but conventional SSL methods developed 
for single-modality data may not adequately capture and eﬀectively 
leverage cross-modal information. To overcome this limitation, SSL 
methods specialized for multimodal imaging have recently been devel-
oped. MultiMAE [43] samples visible patches from multiple modalities 
based on a Dirichlet distribution while masking the rest and then recon-
structs the masked context. Table et al. [44] present a novel SSL method 
for multimodal medical images by solving multimodal jigsaw puzzles. 
M3AE [18] employs a masked autoencoder to reconstruct multimodal 
brain MRI and leverages cross-modal learning to improve representation 
alignment. BrainMVP [19] employs a modality-wise distillation strat-
egy to align and unify representations across multiple MRI modalities. 
However, both M3AE and BrainMVP rely on ﬁxed and shared modality 
templates, which may not fully capture the complex and patient-speciﬁc 
characteristics of brain tumors.
3.  Methods
The goal of our MF-MCM framework is to learn transferable and gen-
eralizable representations from multimodal brain MRI. The pipeline is 
depicted in Fig. 1 and consists of the following key steps: (1) performing 
modality fusion, ensuring that speciﬁc modalities dominate designated 
channels, (2) applying complementary masking to the multichannel MRI 
after modality fusion, and (3) training a model to restore the original 
multimodal MRI while regressing out the fusion coeﬃcients. Each step 
will be explicitly explained in subsequent subsections.
3.1.  MF-MCM paradigm
3.1.1.  Modality fusion
During pre-training, enhanced data diversity facilitates the learning 
of generalizable and robust image representations. However, brain re-
gions of a similar tissue type, such as the hippocampus and the amygdala 
that both belong to subcortical gray matter, often exhibit similar inten-
sity patterns within the same MRI modality, limiting the eﬀectiveness 
of intensity-based perturbations in context restoration tasks. We imple-
ment cross-modality, patch-wise Mix-Up rather than global volume-level 
fusion. This choice is motivated by the need to maximize spatial data 
diversity: by applying distinct fusion coeﬃcients to localized image re-
gions, we prevent the network from relying on global intensity statistics 
and force it to learn robust, ﬁne-grained cross-modal correspondences.

3.1.2.  Masked complementary modeling
While superior performance has been achieved by MIM-based meth-
ods in various medical imaging tasks [37], their masking strategies typ-
ically conceal identical regions in all modalities or apply random masks 
to each modality independently, thus overlooking the intrinsic cross-
modal correspondence during image reconstruction. To overcome this 
limitation, we perform Masked Complementary Modeling (MCM); the 
strategy is derived from our previous work [25]. In simple terms, com-
plementary masking ensures that diﬀerent MRI modalities observe en-
tirely distinct, non-overlapping regions of the brain. Since the network 
must reconstruct the full volume for each modality, it cannot rely solely 
on surrounding pixels within the same modality. This mechanism explic-
itly drives the model to learn the synergistic relationships and structural 
correspondences across diﬀerent MRI modalities.
As illustrated in Fig. 2, we ﬁrst uniformly partition the brain MRI 
into 𝐻ℎ × 𝑊𝑤 × 𝐷𝑑  image patches. Then these patches are randomly di-
vided into four equal portions and assigned to the four fused images 
corresponding to each modality to obtain the complementary represen-
tation.
Subsequently, a binary masking operation is applied to these com-
plementary positions, masking 75% of patches in each channel of ̂𝑋. 
The masked patches are replaced with zeros. Finally, the visible regions 
across the four channels in ̂𝑋 become mutually exclusive and they com-
plementarily constitute the entire brain space. By presenting the model 
with these complementary "puzzle pieces", we encourage it to build 
strong cross-modal bridges rather than relying solely on trivial intra-
modal interpolation.
3.2.  Pre-training
3.2.1.  Network architecture
We conduct comprehensive evaluations of our proposed method us-
ing two distinct backbone architectures: (1) a 3D U-Net [45] based 
on CNN, and (2) a Swin UNETR [41] architecture employing vision 
transformer (ViT). The 3D U-Net implementation follows a symmet-
ric encoder-decoder structure, where both components consist of hi-
erarchically stacked convolutional blocks. These blocks are intercon-
nected through successive downsampling and upsampling operations, 
with skip connections incorporated to enable multi-scale feature aggre-
gation. In contrast, Swin UNETR adopts a hybrid architectural paradigm. 
Its encoder leverages both Window-based Multi-head Self-Attention (W-
MSA) and Shifted Window-based Multi-head Self-Attention (SW-MSA) 
modules to capture long-range dependencies. Spatial downsampling is 
achieved through patch merging operations.

4.  Experiments
4.1.  Datasets
The BraTS2023-Adult Glioma (GLI) dataset [20] is used to pre-train 
our MF-MCM. Speciﬁcally, we randomly select 900 MRI data, with four 
MR modalities, from the GLI dataset and their tumor segmentation labels 
are not used during the pre-training phase. We comprehensively eval-

4.2.  Implementation details
All images in the ﬁve datasets undergo comprehensive pre-
processing, including background cropping, intensity range clipping be-
tween the 1𝑠𝑡 and 99𝑡ℎ percentiles of the original distribution, and then 
normalization to have zero mean and unit variance. During pre-training, 
we apply data augmentation through random spatial transformations, 
including random zooming (with a zooming range from 0.7 to 1.4), 
random cropping (with an image size of 128 × 128 × 128), and random 
rotation (spanning from 30◦ to −30◦) to enlarge the pre-training set. 
For ﬁne-tuning, we additionally incorporate Gamma correction (with a 
range from 0.7 to 1.5) alongside the pre-training augmentation pipeline.
We employ the AdamW optimizer with momentum parameters 𝛽1 =
0.9 and 𝛽2 = 0.999, a weight decay of 1e-5, and a batch size of 4 for 
both the pre-training and ﬁne-tuning phases. The model undergoes 800 
epochs for pre-training and 300 epochs for ﬁne-tuning, with validation 
performed at 10-epoch intervals during ﬁne-tuning to select the best-
performing model. The learning rate follows a cosine decay schedule 
with a 50-epoch warm-up period, initialized at 3e-4 for pre-training and 
1e-4 for ﬁne-tuning. All experiments are performed using PyTorch 2.0.1 
on a workstation equipped with NVIDIA RTX 3090 GPUs. Performance 
is evaluated using the Dice Similarity Coeﬃcient (DSC), 95th percentile 
Hausdorﬀ Distance (HD95), and Average Symmetric Surface Distance 
(ASSD). In cases where a "false positive" occurs (prediction exists but 
ground truth is empty) or a "false negative" occurs (ground truth exists 
but prediction is empty), both HD95 and ASSD are assigned a maximum 
penalty value of 373.12866𝑚𝑚. This value corresponds to the spatial di-
agonal of the image volume in BraTS challenge datasets: 240 × 240 × 155
[20]. We evaluate the brain tumor segmentation performance on three 
clinically relevant subregions: the whole tumor (WT), the tumor core 
(TC, the combination of NET and ET), and the enhancing tumor (ET).
In our extensive experiments, we benchmark a range of SSL meth-
ods that employ CNN (3D-UNet) or ViT (Swin UNETR) as the backbone 
for volumetric medical image pre-training. These methods fall into four 
categories: (1) context restoration based on customized data augmenta-
tion, including Models Genesis [35], (2) context restoration that incor-
porates medical prior, including ASA [37], SwinMM [39], and TransVW 
[38], (3) hybrid methods that combine multiple SSL techniques, includ-
ing PCRLv2 [40] and Tang et al. [8], and (4) multimodal SSL frame-
works designed for multimodal brain MRI imaging, including M3AE [18]

parison methods from scratch using the same pre-training data as that 
used in this study, based on their oﬃcial repositories. Given that sub-
stantially more single-modal medical images are available compared to 
multimodal data, we verify whether pre-training strategies tailored for 
single-modal images shall achieve superior performance by leveraging 
a much larger amount of pre-training data. Therefore, we additionally 
compare the segmentation performance of ASA and SwinMM by ﬁne-
tuning their publicly released pre-trained weights, both pre-trained on 
large-scale single-modal datasets, ASA on 11,993 T1w MRI scans and 
SwinMM on 5833 CT scans. Please note, since PCRLv2 employs a net-
work without skip connections, we maintain this network structure as 
the backbone for this method. Meanwhile, for comparative methods 
originally designed for single-modal data, we adapt them to multimodal 
MRI by stacking all modalities as model input and modifying the in-
put layer to accommodate the four-channel input. When ﬁne-tuing pub-
licly released pre-trained weights of ASA and SwinMM, we replicate 
the weights of the input layer for four times to accommodate the four-
channel input. We refer to models trained from scratch as ’scratch’ in 
the following sections.
5.  Results
5.1.  Evaluation on brain tumor segmentation
5.1.1.  Glioma
GLI. Table 2 details the quantitative comparisons on the GLI dataset 
across DSC, HD95, and ASSD metrics. Methods with strict assumptions 
of anatomical symmetry or cross-subject consistency, such as TransVW 
and ASA, demonstrate marginal improvements or even performance 
degradation compared to scratch. This incompatibility stems from the 
intrinsic morphological heterogeneity of gliomas. Conversely, relaxing 
these assumptions (PCRLv2, Models Genesis) or leveraging perspective-
invariant semantic consistency (SwinMM) aligns better with glioma 
characteristics, yielding clear performance gains. Among the competi-
tors, M3AE and Tang et al. show strong performance under CNN and 
ViT backbones, respectively. Nevertheless, our MF-MCM consistently 
demonstrates superior tumor boundary delineation, an advantage di-
rectly reﬂected in the distance-based metrics. Under the CNN backbone, 
MF-MCM outperforms M3AE by 0.52% in average DSC and reduces the 
average ASSD by a notable 1.75𝑚𝑚 (from 6.35𝑚𝑚 to 4.60𝑚𝑚), while 
achieving a highly competitive average HD95 of 7.92𝑚𝑚 (comparable 
to BrainMVP’s 7.87𝑚𝑚). Under the ViT backbone, MF-MCM achieves 
SOTA performance across all metrics, surpassing Tang et al. with a 
1.13% increase in average DSC, alongside reductions of 0.28𝑚𝑚 and 
0.04𝑚𝑚 in average HD95 and ASSD, respectively. These comprehensive 
improvements validate MF-MCM’s exceptional capability in capturing 
cross-modal relationships to precisely delineate complex tumor bound-
aries.
SSA and PED. Table 3 presents the quantitative evaluation on the 
SSA dataset. When employing 3D-UNet as the backbone, most SSL meth-
ods exhibit consistent performance trends relative to the scratch base-
line across both SSA and GLI datasets, suggesting similar imaging dis-
tributions between adult glioma cohorts of diﬀerent ethnic groups. No-
tably, our MF-MCM maintains a signiﬁcant advantage in boundary pre-
cision. For instance, under the CNN backbone, MF-MCM achieves the 
lowest average HD95 (4.99𝑚𝑚) and ASSD (1.19𝑚𝑚), outperforming the 
competitive M3AE which shows higher distance errors (HD95: 9.87𝑚𝑚; 
ASSD: 2.50𝑚𝑚). However, when switching to the Swin UNETR back-
bone, existing SSL methods show performance degradation compared 
to scratch, whereas MF-MCM demonstrates superior robustness by im-
proving the average DSC to 88.10% and reducing the average HD95 
to 5.67𝑚𝑚. As illustrated in Fig. 3, our MF-MCM eﬀectively segments 
challenging regions, namely ET and NET areas, in both GLI and SSA 
datasets. As shown in Table 4, the domain shift challenge is more pro-
nounced in the PED dataset. We observe that most SOTA SSL methods 
undergo signiﬁcant performance drops compared to the scratch base-
line. This is attributed to the substantial domain discrepancy between 
adult and pediatric gliomas, which diﬀer in both anatomical locations 
(pons vs. frontal/temporal lobes) and imaging characteristics [22]. Al-
though M3AE and BrainMVP are tailored for multimodal brain MRI, 
their reliance on ﬁxed modality templates during pre-training hinders 
their adaptability to pediatric anatomy. In contrast, MF-MCM consis-
tently outperforms all comparative methods across diﬀerent architec-
tures. Under the CNN backbone, it achieves an average DSC of 75.30% 
and a marked reduction in boundary errors (average ASSD: 23.74𝑚𝑚 vs. 
M3AE’s 24.13𝑚𝑚). Even in the ViT-based PED evaluation, where Tang et 
al. and SwinMM show strength in speciﬁc regions, MF-MCM maintains 
the best overall performance with an average DSC of 74.60% and the 
lowest average HD95 of 26.82𝑚𝑚. These results conﬁrm that by eﬀec-
tively capturing cross-modal relationships, MF-MCM exhibits superior 
generalizability across diverse age groups and data distributions.
5.1.2.  Other types of brain tumors
MET. We further assess the segmentation performance on brain 
metastasis (MET). As shown in Table 5, MF-MCM consistently achieves 
superior performance across most metrics. Speciﬁcally, MF-MCM yields 
average DSC improvements of 0.85% (3D-UNet) and 3.19% (Swin UN-
ETR) over the scratch. Similar to the trends observed on the PED dataset, 
most SSL methods exhibit performance degradation, which we attribute 
to the substantial domain discrepancy between MET and GLI, partic-
ularly regarding tumor sites and morphology. In the CNN-based eval-
uation, MF-MCM outpaces M3AE by reducing the average HD95 from 
27.32𝑚𝑚 to 26.16𝑚𝑚 and the average ASSD from 19.04𝑚𝑚 to 18.55𝑚𝑚. 
Under the ViT backbone, while SwinMM achieves a lower average ASSD, 
MF-MCM maintains the highest segmentation overlap (73.25% DSC) 
and the best HD95 of 20.93𝑚𝑚. These results further highlight MF-MCM’s 
ability to eﬀectively capture cross-modal interactions, enabling robust 
generalization across diverse brain tumor types.
Across all four downstream brain tumor segmentation tasks, MF-
MCM consistently achieves the best overall performance, regardless of 
whether the backbone is 3D-UNet or Swin UNETR. It outperforms both 
M3AE and BrainMVP which are speciﬁcally designed for multimodal 
brain MRI, particularly in reducing distance-based errors where the 
baseline methods often struggle. The results further show that SSL meth-
ods for single-modal imaging, even when pre-trained on substantially 
larger single-modal datasets, still fall short of our MF-MCM in brain tu-
mor segmentation. The average DSC values across the four tasks clearly 
reﬂect this trend (ASA: 77.51%, SwinMM: 78.26%, MF-MCM: 80.06%). 
These ﬁndings indicate that for brain tumor segmentation, equipping the 
model with explicit cross-modal interaction and multimodal fusion ca-
pabilities can eﬀectively compensate for the smaller size of multimodal 
pre-training data, ultimately leading to boundary delineation accuracy.
5.2.  Evaluation on brain lesion segmentation
ATLAS2. We evaluate the downstream performance of MF-MCM on 
single-modal T1w MRI for the segmentation of stroke lesions. As shown 
in Table 6, TransVW and ASA underperform the scratch, likely due to 
their underlying assumptions being incompatible with the imaging char-
acteristics of stroke lesions. In contrast, SwinMM outperforms scratch, 
particularly when leveraging open-source weights pre-trained on large-
scale CT scans, demonstrating the beneﬁt of cross-domain knowledge. 
Our MF-MCM demonstrates robust performance across diﬀerent back-
bones, outperforming the best alternative SSL methods by 0.49% (CNN) 
and 2.65% (ViT) in mean DSC. Notably, under the ViT backbone, MF-
MCM achieves SOTA results across all metrics, reducing the average 
HD95 and ASSD to 17.56𝑚𝑚 and 5.58𝑚𝑚, respectively, a signiﬁcant im-
provement over SwinMM leveraging open-source weights. Under the 
CNN backbone, while M3AE exhibits slightly better boundary distances, 
MF-MCM maintains the highest segmentation overlap (61.59% DSC).

5.3.  Ablation study
Ablation analyses are performed on the GLI dataset to investigate 
the mechanism and eﬀectiveness of our method in three aspects: (1) the 
contribution of each component, (2) the eﬀect of the training set size, 
and (3) the impact of the available modalities. All ablation experiments 
are performed using 3D-UNet as the backbone network.
5.3.1.  Eﬃcacy of MF-MCM’s main components
Table 7 presents the results assessing MF-MCM’s main components: 
MCM and MF, evaluated across DSC, HD95, and ASSD metrics. To 
demonstrate the advantages of our method over conventional MIM, we 
implement a random masking baseline that randomly masks 75% of 
regions per modality (the 2nd row). MCM enhances MIM by explicitly 
modeling cross-modal relationships, achieving a 1.15% average DSC im-
provement alongside substantial reductions in boundary errors, decreas-
ing the average HD95 from 15.08𝑚𝑚 to 10.69𝑚𝑚 and the average ASSD 
from 7.41𝑚𝑚 to 6.53𝑚𝑚 (the 2nd row 𝑣𝑠. the 3rd row). The MF component 
strengthens the capture of the cross-modality relationship while enrich-
ing data diversity, and this can be demonstrated by a further 0.40% av-
erage DSC improvement and consistent decreases in the average HD95 
(to 9.16𝑚𝑚) and ASSD (to 6.28𝑚𝑚) (the 3rd row 𝑣𝑠. the 4th row). Fur-
thermore, incorporating the regression objective of 𝑟𝑒𝑔 enhances the 
model’s performance by decoupling modality-speciﬁc components from 
the fused representation. This is clearly evidenced by the overall best 
boundary delineation performance, where the average HD95 and ASSD 
further drop to 7.92𝑚𝑚 and 4.60𝑚𝑚, respectively, while the average DSC 
peaks at 88.68% (the 4th row 𝑣𝑠. the 𝑙𝑎𝑠𝑡 row). These results validate 
our method’s eﬀectiveness in leveraging complementary multimodal in-
formation to improve downstream segmentation accuracy and spatial 
boundary precision.
5.3.2.  Data eﬃciency
To assess data eﬃciency, we conduct comparative experiments be-
tween our method and scratch on varying dataset sizes ranging from 1 to 
210 samples. As illustrated in Fig. 4, MF-MCM demonstrates remarkable 
data eﬃciency: (1) achieving 80.04% average Dice with 10 samples; (2) 
surpassing scratch’s 210-sample performance when trained on 40 sam-
ples; and (3) reaching 88.11% average Dice (merely 0.52% below the 
210-sample performance) with 60 samples. These ﬁndings conclusively 
demonstrate the ability of MF-MCM to maintain high segmentation ac-
curacy while substantially reducing annotation requirements by at least 
40%.

5.3.5.  Scalability to diverse modality scenarios
To evaluate the ﬂexibility and generalizability of MF-MCM, we in-
vestigate whether the framework can eﬀectively learn representations 
when the number of input modalities is varied. Although our default 
paradigm is optimized for four modalities, the core mechanisms of 
modality fusion and masked complementary modeling are theoretically 
scalable to any number (≥ 2) of inputs. To this end, we conduct exten-
sion experiments using dual-modality and triple-modality setups. For 
each scenario, the model is pre-trained and subsequently ﬁne-tuned on 
the corresponding modality subsets. The results in Table 14 demonstrate 
that MF-MCM is ﬂexible with respect to the number of available modali-
ties and scales seamlessly across varied input conﬁgurations. We observe 
that as the number of available modalities during pre-training increases, 
the downstream performance improves consistently.
Speciﬁcally, for the dual-modality conﬁguration, transitioning to 
full-modality pre-training boosts the average DSC to 84.57% and re-
duces the average HD95 to 10.40𝑚𝑚. A similar trend is evident in the 
triple-modality scenario, where full-modality pre-training signiﬁcantly 
reﬁnes ET’s boundary localization. This conﬁrms that, provided with 
richer modality information, MF-MCM captures more comprehensive 
cross-modal synergies and universal anatomical priors, eﬀectively bridg-
ing the information gap for modality-constrained clinical scenarios.

of MF-MCM exhibits highly stable optimization behavior. As illustrated 
in Fig. 5, both the reconstruction loss and the fusion coeﬃcients re-
gression loss decrease smoothly, converging steadily around epoch 500 
without noticeable oscillation. Compared to standard MIM, the addi-
tional modality fusion operation introduces only modest computational 
overhead, while maintaining highly eﬃcient convergence speed. A crit-
ical challenge in multimodal pre-training is preserving ﬁne anatomical 
structures, particularly under heavy masking or Mix-Up operations. In 
our framework, as shown in Fig. 5, the structural integrity in the visi-
ble regions is maintained because the fusion process is asymmetric; for 
each mixed input, one speciﬁc modality acts as the "dominant" modal-
ity, ensuring the primary anatomical topology is preserved rather than 
blurred. Additionally, the complementary masking strategy guarantees 
that every spatial region is visible in at least one modality. As shown 
in Fig. 5, even in regions with severe disease where tumor textures are 
highly heterogeneous, the critical boundary information is successfully 
reconstructed.
To prevent the model from learning trivial or shortcut solutions dur-
ing the fusion coeﬃcients regression task, we speciﬁcally restrict the 
regression loss calculation to the visible patches only. If the regression 
were computed globally, the network could exploit a statistical shortcut 
by predominantly predicting zeros for the 75% masked regions, lead-
ing to severe mode collapse and failing to capture true modality con-
tributions. Predicting fusion coeﬃcients in visible regions also proves 
beneﬁcial for small or rare tumor regions, as the local patch-level re-
gression forces the model to heavily weigh localized pathological fea-
tures rather than relying on global background heuristics. To mitigate 
the risk of overﬁtting during extensive pre-training on limited datasets, 
our modality fusion strategy intrinsically provides massive, highly gran-
ular data augmentation. Mathematically, instead of learning from a dis-
crete set of four standalone modalities, the fusion process in Eq. 1 maps 
the inputs into a highly dense combinatorial space. Although the fusion 
coeﬃcients 𝛿 are sampled from a discrete set with a step size of 0.05, 
this granular distribution provides a rich spectrum of blended intensi-
ties. More importantly, because distinct fusion coeﬃcients are applied 
independently across diﬀerent patches within the same volume, the net-
work encounters an exponentially large variety of modality-mixed vol-
umetric patterns. This extreme spatial data diversity prevents the net-
work from relying on global intensity statistics and eﬀectively regu-
larizes the network over long pre-training schedules, yielding satisfac-
tory downstream performance with fewer than 1000 pre-training cases. 
Furthermore, this paradigm can be naturally extended to consistency-
based semi-supervised frameworks such as Mean Teacher [46]. By ap-
plying diﬀerent fusion coeﬃcients to the same data to generate multiple 
"modality views", the model is encouraged to produce consistent predic-
tions, thereby signiﬁcantly enhancing the reliability of pseudo-labels.
Despite the observed improvements, certain failure cases persist. Ex-
tremely small lesions remain challenging for all evaluated methods, in-
cluding MF-MCM, particularly when certain sub-regions (e.g., the ET 
region) are absent in a minority of training samples, which may lead to 
occasional false positives. Nevertheless, our method demonstrates su-
perior detection capabilities on datasets characterized by small lesions, 
such as MET and ATLAS2, outperforming existing SOTA methods. For 
segmentation tasks with such characteristics, the learned encoder can 
be transferred to segmentation architectures speciﬁcally optimized for 
small target detection.
Despite the promising results, our study has two primary limita-
tions. First, our complementary modeling requires anatomical align-
ment across modalities, which is typically achievable using standard 
registration software like ANTs [47]. We have also analyzed the sce-
nario of pre-training with inaccurately registered datasets. As shown 
in Table 13, MF-MCM exhibits a certain degree of robustness against 
registration inaccuracies. Second, our current pre-training relies on the 
BraTS glioma dataset, potentially limiting diversity and introducing do-
main bias. This bias often manifests as performance instability during 
task transfer. Speciﬁcally, while several SOTA SSL methods suﬀer from 
performance decay and fall behind scratch counterparts in other brain 
tumor tasks beyond glioma, our approach demonstrates superior adapt-
ability. By maintaining high performance across various pathological 
conditions, MF-MCM proves more resilient to the challenges of cross-
domain deployment than existing SSL paradigms. As neuroimaging shar-
ing platforms like the BraTS series continue to grow, our framework can 
be incrementally updated with more heterogeneous data. Experiments 
with reduced modality subsets in Table 14 further suggest that the model 
maintains adaptability under varying acquisition conditions, supporting 
its potential for generalizability. Training on a broader spectrum of neu-
rological conditions and diverse scanner protocols will likely enhance 
the robustness of our strategy. Furthermore, the modality fusion mech-
anism itself serves as an implicit domain generalization tool. By generat-
ing a nearly continuous spectrum of "unseen" modality contrasts during 
pre-training, the model learns to be more invariant to intensity shifts 
caused by diﬀerent scanners or imaging protocols, thereby improving 
its clinical utility in varied research settings.
While our experiments primarily focus on multimodal brain MRI, 
the proposed MF-MCM framework is not inherently restricted to this 
domain. Provided that spatial co-registration is feasible, the complemen-
tary masking and modality fusion mechanisms can be readily extended 
to other multimodal medical imaging tasks, such as PET-MRI or CT-
MRI. Regarding computational cost and practical trade-oﬀs, MF-MCM is 
highly eﬃcient and architecture-agnostic. The modality fusion and com-
plementary masking operations introduce only modest computational 
overhead compared to standard MIM pipelines. To demonstrate its ar-
chitectural ﬂexibility, we evaluate MF-MCM on backbones with vastly 
diﬀerent parameter scales: a lightweight CNN (3D-UNet, ∼18.92M pa-
rameters) and a heavier Transformer (Swin UNETR, ∼62.19M parame-
ters). MF-MCM consistently achieves SOTA performance across both ar-
chitectures, proving that it does not rely on massive model capacities to 
succeed. Most importantly, these robust, generalizable representations 
are acquired using fewer than 1000 unannotated pre-training cases. This 
exceptional data eﬃciency enables researchers and clinicians to make 
ﬂexible trade-oﬀs between computational cost and model capacity dur-
ing practical deployment.
We speciﬁcally choose MIM as our primary baseline, as it has 
been widely recognized and validated for its eﬀectiveness in learn-
ing ﬁne-grained structural representations from unannotated medical 
data. Furthermore, our comparisons include recent SOTA approaches 
like BrainMVP [19], which is built upon the MIM paradigm but in-
troduces modality-speciﬁc templates and modality-aware contrastive 
learning. Moving forward, MF-MCM could be further enhanced by incor-
porating advanced strategies from these recent works; for instance, the 
anatomical-consistency-driven contrastive learning employed in Brain-
MVP could be integrated as an auxiliary objective to further strengthen 
MF-MCM’s global cross-modality alignment and feature discriminative-
ness.
7.  Conclusion
In this paper, we introduce a novel SSL method for multimodal brain 
MRI, named MF-MCM. Two innovative strategies, Modality Fusion and 
Masked Complementary Modeling, are designed to capture cross-modal 
interaction information and enable eﬀective integration of complemen-
tary information across modalities. We validate the eﬀectiveness of MF-
MCM in four brain tumor segmentation tasks and a brain lesion segmen-
tation task. MF-MCM consistently outperforms representative SSL meth-
ods. Ablation studies emphasize the importance of the main components 
and demonstrate the robustness of MF-MCM to insuﬃcient training data 
and incomplete modalities. Future work will focus on extending MF-
MCM to a broader range of multimodal medical images and exploring 
additional strategies to further enhance the model’s robustness. Further-
more, the MF-MCM framework oﬀers seamless architectural scalability 
regarding the number of input modalities.
